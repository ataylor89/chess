# A chess lesson

My last post made me feel a little self-conscious. So I'm going to hide behind a chess lesson :)

I have been passionate about chess for a long time, since childhood.

But honestly, you can discover a passion at any age. (And maybe you'll find out... you had this passion at the beginning of time.)

One theory is that we were all born, as spirits, at the beginning of time.

I'm a big believer in spiritual life.

Now, back to the chess lesson.

I think it helps to dive right in. So, what is my approach to learning chess?

My approach to learning chess is this: I study every part of the game, and I try to master every part of the game.

I like to divide chess into five parts: openings, middlegames, endgames, tactics, and strategy.

I have various sources for learning openings (books, YouTube videos, Chessable, chessdotcom's analysis feature, chessdotcom's lessons feature, watching famous chess tournaments like the World Chess Championship or Tata Steel, and watching chess streams).

It took me a long time, but I built an opening repertoire out of openings that I really like.

It helps to play openings that you enjoy.

Now, middlegames are so connected to openings, that we can even think of them as a subset of opening theory.

I like to say that the opening ends when a player finishes their development. But I don't know how other people define it.

It's also the case that middlegames are connected to tactics and strategy: you learn middlegame ideas by studying tactics and strategy.

You could even draw connections between middlegames and endgames: some middlegames aim for a favorable endgame.

So, we can divide chess into five parts (openings, middlegames, endgames, tactics, strategy).

We can also divide chess into four parts (openings, endgames, tactics, strategy).

I think that both divisions are useful.

You can see that I learn middlegame positions by studying the other four parts: openings, endgames, tactics, and strategy.

Now with endgames I can be more concrete.

I learned endgames by reading Bruce Pandolfini's book on endgames, Endgame Workshop.

I also learned endgames by reading Mark Dvoretsky's Endgame Manual.

Keep in mind that you don't have to read a book cover to cover.

I did not read Mark Dvoretsky's book cover to cover, but I read parts of it.

I may have read Bruce Pandolfini's book cover to cover, but I don't remember, to be honest.

These two books really helped me increase my knowledge of the endgame.

I also learned endgames by studying the games of Magnus Carlsen.

Magnus Carlsen is famous for outplaying his opponent in an equal endgame, and going on to win it.

I really enjoy following Magnus Carlsen's games. I really enjoy following Magnus Carlsen's chess career.

In fact, in the year 2018, I attended the World Chess Championship in London.

I was actually in the same room as Magnus Carlsen and Fabiano Caruana, and I saw them play from behind a glass screen.

I stood next to Sergey Karjakin in the hallway.

I saw Daniel King, and was in the same room as Daniel King. We were both in a room where many spectators gathered to play or talk or kibitz. I was able to recognize him because I have seen him commentate many tournaments that I had watched online.

I met Judit Polgar, and had a brief conversation with her. I asked her how to improve at chess. She gave me some useful pointers.

I met Eric Rosen at the venue, too. It was actually my second time meeting him.

So you can tell, I'm very proud of all the people I met at the 2018 World Chess Championship in London.

Let's return to the subject of improving at chess.

Let's pick up where I left off.

I learned endgames by studying the games of Magnus Carlsen.

That covers the OME in OMETS.

What about tactics and strategy?

Well, I have various sources for learning tactics and strategy.

I learned tactics and strategy by reading Mikhail Tal's My Life and Games and Bobby Fischer's My 60 Memorable Games.

I learned tactics by reading Yasser Seirawan's Winning Chess Tactics.

I learned strategy by reading Yasser Seirawan's Winning Chess Strategies.

I learned tactics by doing the Rated Puzzles on chessdotcom.

I learned tactics by doing Puzzle Rushes on chessdotcom.

I learned strategy by taking Jeremy Silman's lessons on positional chess, Roots of Positional Understanding 1 & 2, on chessdotcom.

I also learned tactics by doing puzzles on the DuoLingo chess app.

You can see that I have diverse sources for learning tactics and strategy.

In fact, I have diverse sources for learning all five parts of the game: openings, middlegames, endgames, tactics, and strategy.

I think the main message in this post is this:

I learn chess by studying every part of the game (openings, middlegames, endgames, tactics, and strategy).

I try to master every part of the game.

I have diverse sources for learning the different parts of the game, many of which I have shared above.

I think that all three statements comprise a single message.

But a secondary, and equally important message, is this:

You can start chess at any age. Rating doesn't matter... what matters is your enjoyment of the game.

Chess teaches us how to evaluate a position. Chess teaches us how to find the best move.

Chess is a metaphor for decision making. In chess, we try to make the best moves. In life, we try to make the best decisions.

We can think of every chess move as a decision.

And with that, I'll end this chess lesson. :)
